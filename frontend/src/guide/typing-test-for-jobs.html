How to prepare for a typing test for a job

Last updated November 27, 2026

Jobs with a lot of typing, such as admin, data entry, customer support and transcription, sometimes ask applicants to take a timed typing test. This guide explains what those tests can involve, how their numbers relate to the ones PuggieType shows, and how to spend one or two weeks practicing for one.

Please note: PuggieType is a free practice tool. It does not issue typing certificates, a result here is not a verified score, and PuggieType has no connection to any employer, recruiter or testing service. This page is general practice advice, not hiring advice, and it can't tell you whether you will pass a particular test.

What employer typing tests can involve

There is no single standard. Each employer, or the service it uses, sets its own rules, and two tests for similar jobs can work quite differently. The job posting or the email inviting you to the test usually explains the format. If it doesn't, it is reasonable to ask. These are the things worth finding out.

  • How long it lasts. Some tests are a single minute, others run for several. Length matters because speed often drops over a longer test.
  • What you type. You might copy a passage of normal sentences with capitals and punctuation, type a list of random words, or enter realistic material such as names, addresses and order numbers.
  • Whether backspace is allowed. Some tests let you fix mistakes freely. Some disable backspace. Some let you correct but still count the original error.
  • Gross or net speed. A gross speed counts everything you typed. A net speed takes your errors into account. The next section explains the difference.
  • An accuracy requirement. Some tests set a minimum accuracy as well as a minimum speed, and a fast result with too many errors can fail.
  • Numeric or 10-key tests. Data entry roles may test how quickly you enter numbers on the number pad. These results are sometimes reported in keystrokes per hour rather than words per minute.
  • Where you take it. At home in your own browser, or at the employer's office on a keyboard you have never used.

Requirements for speed and accuracy also vary from job to job. Look for them in the posting rather than relying on a figure you read somewhere else.

Gross speed, net speed and PuggieType's numbers

PuggieType, like many typing tests, counts every five characters, spaces included, as one word. Where tests differ is in how mistakes are handled.

  • Gross WPM counts all the characters you typed, right or wrong, and turns them into words per minute.
  • Net WPM starts from the gross figure and takes something off for errors. One common approach subtracts a penalty for each uncorrected error; others scale the speed by your accuracy. The exact rule depends on the test, and the instructions or the person running it can tell you which one applies.

On PuggieType, the big wpm number counts only the characters of words you typed completely correctly, plus the space after each. A word with one wrong letter adds nothing. That makes it a net-style figure: errors you leave in the text pull it down. It is not identical to any particular employer's formula, though. An error here costs the whole word, so a typo in a long word costs more than one in a short word, instead of a fixed penalty per error.

raw is the closer match to gross. It counts every correct, incorrect and extra character left in your text, whether or not the word was right.

Accuracy needs care too. PuggieType's acc counts every wrong keypress at the moment you press it, even one you fix straight away. Some employer tests only count errors that are still in the text at the end. So your PuggieType accuracy may read lower than the same typing would score on such a test. Neither is wrong; they measure different things. The WPM and accuracy guide shows the exact formulas.

In practice: if a job asks for a certain net speed, compare it with your PuggieType WPM, taken on text similar to theirs and over the same length. Aim to reach it with some room to spare, since test-day nerves and an unfamiliar keyboard can cost you a little.

A one-to-two-week preparation plan

Short sessions on most days work better than one long session the night before. Twenty to thirty minutes is plenty. Stretch the plan over two weeks if you have the time, or compress it into one.

Days 1 and 2: find your starting point

  1. Open the typing test and pick time mode. Choose 60 or 120 in the bar above the words. If the employer's test has a different length, click the tools icon after 120 (on a phone, open test settings and tap custom next to the time lengths) and type it in: "180" or "3m" both give three minutes.
  2. After a short warm-up, take three tests and keep the middle WPM and its accuracy. The 1-minute typing test guide explains this method.
  3. Then switch to quote mode, pick medium or long, and take three more. Real sentences with capitals and punctuation are usually slower than random lowercase words. The difference tells you how much work the symbol keys need.
  4. Open the words history on the result screen (the icon of left-aligned lines in the row under your result; hover over an icon to see its name) and note which words and keys went wrong.

Days 3 to 6: practice the kind of text you will be tested on

  • If you will copy a passage, use quote mode. Quotes are real passages with natural punctuation and capital letters. You can choose short, medium, long or thicc (very long), or search for a specific quote.
  • If you will type random words, stay in time mode and turn on the punctuation and numbers toggles on the left of the bar if the employer's test includes them.
  • If you will enter job-like material, use custom mode. Click custom, then change, and paste or write text that looks like the job: a short customer email, a list of addresses, product codes, invoice lines. Make up your own examples; you don't need real records to practice their shape. Pick repeat to type it in order until the time runs out, or random or shuffle to mix it up, then set a time under limit to match the employer's test length (simple types your text once, with no time limit). If your text has line breaks, you press enter at each one, or, under replace new lines with spaces, click space.
  • If there is a numeric test, put strings of numbers into custom mode, such as 4021 77.50 1983 600.25 12, and type them on the number pad. PuggieType accepts number pad keys as input, but it has no dedicated 10-key test and shows the result as WPM.
  • After each test, use the Practice words button on the result screen (the warning triangle) to retype the words you missed. Two or three rounds a day keeps the same mistakes from settling in.

Days 7 to 10: practice under the employer's rules

The last few days: rehearse

Set everything up to match the real test as closely as you can: same length, same kind of text, same rules. Take two or three full-length tests a day at a pace you can control, and log the WPM and accuracy. Keep the day before light. A few easy tests are enough; a long, tiring session the night before rarely helps.

Accuracy before speed

Employer tests tend to reward clean typing. Net scoring takes errors off your speed, accuracy requirements can fail a fast result, and on tests without backspace every slip stays in the text. Pushing past the pace you can control often lowers the number that counts.

Use your PuggieType results to find your controlled pace. If raw is well above WPM, many words are being lost to small slips, and slowing down slightly will often raise your WPM. If accuracy keeps dipping below the level the job asks for, spend a few sessions on the accuracy guide before doing any speed work. It shows how to find your personal error patterns and drill them.

Test-day tips

  • Settle in first. Arrive or log in a few minutes early. Adjust the chair and screen, rest your hands on the home row, and take a couple of slow breaths.
  • Check the keyboard and browser. At home, use the keyboard you practiced on, close other tabs and silence notifications, and make sure caps lock is off and your system keyboard language is the one you expect. On an unfamiliar keyboard, type a few lines first if you are allowed, to find the backspace and shift keys.
  • Read the rules on screen. Even if you asked beforehand, check the length, whether backspace works, how errors are counted, and whether you get more than one attempt.
  • Warm up beforehand. If you are at home, two or three easy tests on PuggieType loosen your fingers. Don't try to set a personal record right before the real thing.
  • Type at your practice pace. Adrenaline makes it tempting to go faster than usual. Hold the pace you rehearsed.
  • Fix mistakes within the word. If backspace is allowed, correct an error while you are still in that word. Going back several words costs time and rhythm.
  • Recover from a bad start. If the first lines go badly, don't rush to catch up. That usually causes more errors. Let the mistake go, find your rhythm again at the next word, and keep going. On a longer test, a shaky first few seconds matters less than it feels.

If you don't reach the required score yet

It happens, and it is fixable with time. A few things that help:

  • Find out which part fell short. Speed and accuracy need different practice. If you were fast but inaccurate, slow down and work on errors. If you were accurate but slow, you have a solid base to build speed on.
  • Ask about retakes. Some employers allow another attempt after a while, and some don't. It is fine to ask politely.
  • Keep a routine going. The typing tips page has a 15-minute daily routine, and the structured plan to type faster runs over several weeks. If you still look at the keys, learning to touch type with the touch typing guide usually helps more than speed drills.
  • Be honest about your speed. If an application asks for your typing speed, give a number you can reproduce under test conditions, not your single highest result.
  • Look after your hands. More practice only helps if it stays comfortable. The typing ergonomics guide has general tips on chair, desk and breaks.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a PuggieType result as proof of my typing speed?

No. PuggieType is a practice tool. It does not issue typing certificates, and its results are not verified scores for employers. If an employer needs proof of your speed, they will usually tell you which test to take.

Is PuggieType's WPM a net or a gross speed?

It is closer to net. WPM counts only words you typed completely correctly, so errors left in the text lower it. Raw counts everything you typed and is closer to a gross figure. An employer's net formula may work differently, so check their instructions.

How long should I practice before a job typing test?

Short daily sessions over one or two weeks give you time to get used to the format and settle your accuracy. Big changes in raw speed usually take longer, so spend that time matching the test and typing cleanly.

Can I practice a test where backspace is disabled?

Yes. Set confidence mode to max in the settings. Backspace then does nothing, so every keypress is final, as it is on the strictest tests.

Can I practice for a numeric or 10-key test?

PuggieType has no dedicated 10-key test, but the number pad works as input. Paste strings of numbers into custom mode and type them on the number pad. Results are shown as a speed in WPM, not as keystrokes per hour.
