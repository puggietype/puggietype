Typing ergonomics: posture, desk setup and breaks

How you sit and how your desk is arranged make a big difference to how an hour at the keyboard feels. This page covers the practical basics: chair, desk, keyboard, screen, breaks, and how to practice without straining yourself.

First, a note about pain

This guide is general information, not medical advice, and we are not health professionals. If you have pain, numbness, tingling, weakness or loss of grip in your hands, wrists, arms, neck or back, stop typing practice and see a doctor or another qualified health professional. Do not try to push through it, and do not rely on a new keyboard or a new posture to fix it on its own. A professional can look at your situation in a way no web page can.

The goal: neutral and relaxed

Almost all ergonomic advice comes down to one idea. Your joints should sit in roughly the middle of their range, not bent hard in any direction, and your muscles should not be holding you up against a bad position. A "neutral" posture is not a single perfect shape you lock into. It is a comfortable starting point you return to, and moving around it is fine and even helpful.

A useful check is to drop your arms by your sides, relax your shoulders, then bend your elbows and bring your hands forward. Wherever your hands land with no effort is close to where the keyboard should be.

Chair and desk

Chair height

Set the chair first. Your feet should rest flat on the floor, or on a footrest if the chair has to be high to reach the desk, with your knees roughly level with your hips. Sit back so the chair supports your lower back; if it has adjustable lumbar support, move it to the small of your back. If your chair has armrests that push your shoulders up or stop you getting close to the desk, lower them or take them off.

Desk height

The keyboard should sit at about the height of your elbows when your shoulders are relaxed, so your forearms are close to level. Many desks are a little too high for this, which makes people lift their shoulders or bend their wrists up. If you can't lower the desk, raise the chair and add a footrest, or use a keyboard tray that sits below the desktop.

Standing desks

A standing desk is not automatically better than sitting; standing all day has its own discomforts. Its real value is that you can change position during the day. The same rule applies standing as sitting: keyboard at elbow height, screen at eye level.

The keyboard

Distance and centering

Keep the keyboard close enough that you are not reaching forward, with your elbows near your sides. Center the part you use most, which for most people is the letter keys rather than the whole board. On a full-size keyboard with a number pad, that means the keyboard sits slightly to the left, so the mouse ends up further right than you might expect.

Tilt

Many keyboards have little feet at the back that raise the far edge. They make the top row easier to see, but they also bend your wrists backwards if your hands are above the keyboard. Try it flat. Some people prefer a slight negative tilt, with the far edge lower than the near edge, which keeps the wrists straight when the keyboard is below elbow height. There is no single right answer: look at your wrists from the side while typing and aim for a straight line from forearm to knuckles.

Wrists and palm rests

Wrists should float while you type, moving with your hands rather than anchoring them in one place while your fingers stretch. A palm rest is for resting during pauses, not for leaning on while typing. If you do rest on it, rest the heel of your palm, not the soft underside of the wrist. Also avoid a sharp desk edge digging into your forearms.

A light touch

Most keys register well before they hit the bottom. Pressing hard and slamming each key to the end of its travel adds force to every one of thousands of keypresses. Try typing as lightly as you can while still registering every key. Watching your accuracy on a few tests helps you find the point where a lighter touch starts to cause missed keys.

Keyboard types

The shape of the keyboard matters less than how you sit at it, but some designs do let you get into a more neutral position. Each has tradeoffs:

Type What it changes Tradeoff
compact (no number pad) Brings the mouse closer to your body. No number pad, which matters if you enter a lot of numbers.
split Each half can sit in front of its shoulder, so your wrists bend outward less. Takes time to adjust; some keys may move to a different hand.
tented Raises the inner edges so your hands are partly turned, like a handshake. Usually combined with a split; often more expensive.
low-profile A thinner keyboard puts less height under your wrists. Shorter key travel, which some people like and some don't.
ortholinear / column-staggered Keys in straight columns, which some people find more natural. A real relearning period, and harder to switch back and forth.

None of these is a cure for anything. If your current keyboard is comfortable, there is no need to replace it. If you are considering a big change, try to type on one before buying, and expect a few weeks of being slower.

Mouse, screen and light

Mouse

Put the mouse right next to the keyboard, at the same height, so you are not reaching sideways or forwards for it. Many people find it more comfortable to move it from the elbow rather than pivoting only at the wrist. Learning a few keyboard shortcuts for things you do often cuts down on mouse use altogether.

Screen

Put the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away, straight in front of you. If you look down at a screen all day, your neck holds your head up at an angle. If you wear glasses with progressive lenses, a slightly lower screen may be more comfortable. With two screens, put the one you use most directly in front of you.

Laptops

A laptop is a compromise: when the keyboard is at the right height, the screen is too low, and when the screen is high enough, the keyboard is too high. For anything longer than a short session, raise the laptop on a stand or a stack of books and use a separate keyboard and mouse.

Light and glare

Avoid sitting with a bright window directly behind the screen or directly behind you, where it reflects. Glare makes people lean in and squint. Keep the screen brightness close to the brightness of the room, and make the text large enough to read without leaning forward. On PuggieType you can change the font size and theme in the settings if the test text is hard to read.

Breaks and movement

The best posture is your next one. Even a good position becomes tiring if you hold it for hours. A few habits help:

  • Micro-pauses. Let your hands drop to your lap for a few seconds whenever you stop to think, instead of hovering over the keys.
  • Short breaks. Every so often, stand up, walk a little, roll your shoulders and look at something far away to rest your eyes. Many people use a simple timer as a reminder.
  • Vary the task. Mix typing with reading, calls or anything that uses your hands differently.
  • Notice tension. Raised shoulders, a clenched jaw or tight forearms are signs to pause and reset.

Practicing without straining

Typing practice is more intense than everyday typing. You are trying to go fast, often in short bursts, and it is easy to tense up without noticing. Some habits for practice sessions:

  • Warm up. Start with a few easy tests at a relaxed pace before pushing for speed. Zen mode is good for this.
  • Keep sessions short. Several short sessions spread over the week are usually easier on your hands, and better for progress, than one long one.
  • Stop when you tense up. If you catch yourself gripping, hunching or hammering the keys to chase a number, take a break. Tension also makes you less accurate, so you lose little by stopping.
  • Do not push through discomfort. Aching after practice is a reason to rest and, if it continues, to get advice, not to practice harder.

Racing is fun but tempts you to tense up and force speed. Mix races with calmer solo practice, and remember that a smooth, relaxed pace usually gives a better result than a strained one.

A quick checklist

  1. Feet flat, knees about level with hips, back supported.
  2. Shoulders relaxed, elbows by your sides.
  3. Keyboard at elbow height, close, letter keys centered.
  4. Wrists straight and floating, light touch on the keys.
  5. Mouse next to the keyboard at the same height.
  6. Screen at eye level, an arm's length away, no glare.
  7. Move often, and stop and get advice if anything hurts.
