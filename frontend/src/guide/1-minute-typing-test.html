The 1-minute typing test: how to take it and read your score

Last updated November 27, 2026

A 1-minute typing test gives you 60 seconds to type as many correct words as you can. It is long enough to smooth out a lucky or unlucky few seconds, and short enough to repeat several times in one sitting. That makes it a good way to track your speed over weeks. This page shows how to start a 60-second test on PuggieType, what each number on the result screen means, and how to measure yourself in a way you can trust.

What a 1-minute test is good for

Think of the 60-second test as a ruler. You use the same ruler every time, so when the number changes, it is more likely that you changed. That is its main job: a repeatable baseline you can come back to once a week and compare.

A minute is also long enough that small accidents matter less. On PuggieType, a word with one wrong letter adds nothing to your WPM. Leave a typo in a six-letter word and you lose seven characters, the word plus its space. In a 15-second test that costs 5.6 WPM. In a 60-second test the same slip costs 1.4, because the loss is spread over four times as much time. The typing test math guide works through this in more detail.

What a 1-minute test is not: a stamina test. Sixty seconds doesn't tell you how your speed holds up while you write a long email or copy a page of notes. Many people start fast and fade, and a minute is often too short for the fade to show clearly. If that is what you want to know, use a 120-second test, a long quote, or a custom length, as described below.

How to start a 60-second test on PuggieType

PuggieType opens on a 30-second test, so you need to change one thing. On a computer:

  1. Open the typing test.
  2. Look at the bar above the words. In the middle are the modes: time, words, quote, zen and custom. Click time. It is selected already unless you changed it before.
  3. To the right of the modes are the time lengths: 15, 30, 60 and 120. Click 60. The words reset and the test is ready.
  4. Start typing. The timer starts with your first keypress, not when the page loads, so you can take a moment to read the first line.
  5. Keep going until the timer reaches zero. The result screen opens by itself.

On a phone or a narrow window the bar is hidden. Use the test settings button above the words instead. It opens the same choices: pick time, then 60.

The bar fades out while you type and while the result is showing, and comes back when you start a new test. To start one, press tab then enter, or click Next test under your result. Each new test uses fresh words. The Repeat test button gives you the same words again, which is useful for practice but not for measuring, because the result is marked as repeated.

On the left of the bar are two toggles, punctuation and numbers. Both are off by default. Decide whether you want them, then leave them alone. A test with commas, capitals and digits is harder, and its score is not comparable with one without.

What each number on the result screen means

The result screen shows more than one number, and each answers a different question. Here is what they are, in short. The WPM and accuracy guide has the exact formulas with examples.

  • wpm is the big number. It counts the characters of words you typed completely correctly, plus the space after each, divides by five, and divides by the test length in minutes. In a 60-second test the length is one minute, so your WPM is just your correct-word characters divided by five.
  • acc is accuracy: the share of your keypresses that were right, counted as you typed. A typo you fixed still counts against it, because the wrong key was pressed. The number on screen is rounded down, so 96.9% shows as 96%.
  • raw is your speed if every character you typed counted, including wrong and extra letters. It shows how fast your fingers moved. The gap between raw and WPM is roughly what mistakes cost you.
  • characters shows four counts separated by slashes: correct, incorrect, extra and missed. Hover over it to see the labels.
  • consistency shows how steady your pace was from one second to the next, from 0 to 100%. A steady typist scores high. Bursts and pauses pull it down.
  • test type lists the mode and length (for example "time 60"), the language, and punctuation or numbers if they were on. Check it before you write a result down.
  • time is how long the test took. For a 60-second test it should show 60 seconds.

The chart

Below the numbers is a chart with one point per second. The wpm and raw lines are running totals: at each second they show your speed so far, which is why they jump around at the start and settle later. The burst line shows how fast you typed in each second on its own, and small marks show the seconds in which you made errors. Hover over any point to see the values. A burst line that drifts down means you started faster than you could keep up. Errors that bunch at the start mean you rushed out of the gate. The typing test math guide explains how each series is built.

If the test was too short, you stopped typing for the last few seconds, or accuracy fell below 75%, the "other" line on the result screen flags it, for example as invalid or too short. Don't use those tests as measurements.

A worked example

Say you take a 60-second test with punctuation and numbers off. When it ends, the words you typed completely correctly add up to 310 characters, spaces included. You also typed 15 correct letters inside words that had a mistake somewhere, 9 wrong characters you didn't fix, and 1 extra letter. 

wpm: 310 / 5 = 62 words in 1 minute = 62 wpm
raw: 310 + 15 + 9 + 1 = 335 characters
     335 / 5 = 67 raw

While typing, you pressed 340 character keys, and 15 of them were wrong at the moment you pressed them: the 9 still in the text, the 1 extra letter, and 5 you fixed along the way. 

accuracy: 325 / 340 x 100 = 95.59%, shown as 95%

What does this tell you? Your fingers moved at 67, but you kept 62. The five missing words per minute went to words with an uncorrected slip. Accuracy around 95% says mistakes are frequent enough to be worth working on before chasing more speed.

15, 30, 60 or 120 seconds: which to use

Each length is useful for something different. The mistake is to mix them up and compare a result from one with a result from another.

Length Good for Keep in mind
15 seconds warming up, short bursts, quick drills Reads high and swings the most. One slip moves it a lot.
30 seconds the default; quick checks and practice sets Fine for practice. Still short enough to flatter you.
60 seconds your weekly baseline Long enough to be steady, short enough to repeat.
120 seconds endurance and focus Compare with your 60-second result to see how much you fade.

For any other length, click the tools icon after 120 in the bar. You can type a number of seconds, or use "m" for minutes and "h" for hours, so "5m" gives you a five-minute test. Timed tests shorter than 15 seconds are marked as too short.

A fair way to measure yourself

One test is a sample, not a measurement. To get a number you can compare from week to week, follow the same steps every time.

  1. Write down your setup once. For example: time mode, 60 seconds, English, punctuation off, numbers off. Use exactly this for every measurement. Same keyboard too, if you can.
  2. Warm up for two or three minutes. A few easy 15-second tests or some free typing in zen mode. Don't look at these scores.
  3. Take three 60-second tests in a row. Use tab then enter or Next test between them, so each one has fresh words.
  4. Keep the middle result. Sort the three WPM numbers and take the one in the middle. With 58, 64 and 61, your result for the day is 61. Note the accuracy of that same test.
  5. Don't cherry-pick. If a test goes badly, it still counts, unless something outside the test interrupted you. If you restart one halfway, take another in its place.

The middle result, the median, barely moves when one test is lucky or unlucky. It mostly moves when your typing changes, which is exactly what you want to see.

Why scores vary from test to test

It is normal for two tests taken a minute apart to differ by a few words per minute. Some of the reasons:

  • The words are different. Each test draws new random words. Some sets have more long words or awkward letter pairs than others.
  • Mistakes land differently. An uncorrected typo in a long word costs more than one in a short word, because the whole word is lost.
  • You are warmer, or more tired. The first test of a session is often slower. After a long session, accuracy tends to slip.
  • Attention wanders. A moment of thinking about something else shows up as a dip in the chart.
  • The setup changed. A different keyboard, chair, language, word list or toggle all change the result.

So don't read too much into one result. One high score is not your new level, and one low score doesn't mean you got worse. A useful habit is to notice how much your own three tests usually differ from each other. If this week's median moved by less than that, treat it as noise and wait for the next few weeks.

Using a weekly 1-minute check alongside practice

The 60-second test measures. It doesn't train much by itself. If all you do is take test after test, you mostly repeat the habits you already have. Keep the measurement and the practice separate.

  • Practice on most days. The typing tips page has a 15-minute routine that ends with a single 60-second test.
  • Measure once a week. Same day, same setup, the three test method above. Keep a short log: date, median WPM, accuracy of that test, and one line on how it felt.
  • Let the log decide what to work on. If raw is well above WPM, or accuracy is often below 95%, spend a week on the accuracy guide. If accuracy is steady and the median has not moved for several weeks, the structured plan to type faster has drills and ways to break a plateau.
  • Look at a month, not a day. Progress is easier to see over four or more weekly checks than in any single one.

If your hands, wrists or shoulders feel uncomfortable during practice, take a break and read the typing ergonomics guide for general setup tips.

Frequently asked questions

How do I take a 1-minute typing test on PuggieType?

Open the typing test, click time in the bar above the words, then click 60. Start typing when you are ready. The timer begins with your first keypress and the result screen opens when it reaches zero.

Is a 60-second typing test the same as a 1-minute test?

Yes. Both names describe the same thing: a timed test that ends after 60 seconds. Because the test lasts exactly one minute, your WPM is simply the characters of your correct words, spaces included, divided by five.

Why is my 1-minute score lower than my 15-second score?

A short test lets you type at a pace you can only hold for a few seconds, and it can end before the first hard word appears. Over a full minute your speed settles to what you can keep up. The 60-second number is the more honest one.

How many tests should I take to know my speed?

Warm up, take three 60-second tests with the same settings, and keep the middle result. Do that on a few different days before you treat the number as your current speed.

Does a 1-minute typing test include punctuation?

Only if you turn it on. Punctuation and numbers are two toggles next to the mode buttons, and both are off by default. Pick one setup and keep it, because results with and without punctuation are not comparable.
