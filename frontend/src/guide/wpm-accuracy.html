How WPM, raw, accuracy and consistency are calculated

The result screen shows four main numbers. Here is exactly what each one measures, how PuggieType computes it, and a worked example you can check with a calculator.

The basic unit: five characters

Words in English have very different lengths, so counting real words would make a test full of "a" and "the" look faster than one full of "keyboard". Typing tests avoid this by treating every five characters as one word, spaces included. All speeds on PuggieType come from one formula: 

speed (wpm) = (characters / 5) / (seconds / 60)

The four results differ only in which characters they count, or, for accuracy and consistency, in what they compare.

How each typed character is classified

When the test ends, every word you typed is compared with the word you were shown, letter by letter, including the space that ends it. Each character falls into one of four groups:

  • correct: the character matches the target;
  • incorrect: you typed a different character in that position;
  • extra: you typed past the end of the word, for example "pugss" instead of "pugs";
  • missed: the target had a character you never typed, for example "pug" instead of "pugs".

These four counts appear on the result screen as "characters", in that order.

WPM: correctly typed words only

WPM, the big number, counts only the characters of words you typed completely correctly, plus the space after each of them. If a word has even one wrong letter when you move on, none of its characters count toward WPM. The last word of a timed test is the exception: if the timer runs out halfway through a word and what you have typed so far is right, those letters still count.

Worked example

You take a 30-second test. The correctly typed words add up to 150 characters, spaces included. 

150 / 5 = 30 words
30 seconds = 0.5 minutes
30 / 0.5 = 60 wpm

Raw: everything you typed

Raw speed ignores whether words were right. It counts every correct, incorrect and extra character, whether or not the word around it was correct. Missed characters are not counted, because you never typed them. Raw tells you how fast your fingers were moving; WPM tells you how much of that motion produced correct text.

Worked example

In the same 30-second test you also typed 10 correct letters inside words that had a mistake somewhere, 8 incorrect characters and 2 extra characters. 

150 + 10 + 8 + 2 = 170 characters
170 / 5 = 34 words
34 / 0.5 = 68 raw

A gap between WPM and raw is normal. A large gap, say 60 WPM against 80 raw, usually means you are rushing and losing whole words to small slips.

Accuracy: every keypress counts

Accuracy is not worked out from the final text. Instead, PuggieType records each character key you press as it happens, marks it correct or incorrect at that moment, and divides: 

accuracy = correct keypresses / (correct + incorrect keypresses) x 100

Spaces count as keypresses. A space pressed in the wrong place is a mistake. Backspace is not counted either way, so fixing a typo does not undo it: the wrong key you pressed is still in the count. That is why you can finish with perfect text and still see 95% accuracy.

Worked example

You pressed 180 character keys. 171 were correct and 9 were wrong, and you went back and fixed all 9. 

171 / 180 x 100 = 95% accuracy

Consistency: how steady your pace was

Consistency measures how much your speed jumped around during the test. PuggieType splits the test into one-second slices, counts the keys you pressed in each slice, and turns each count into a speed with the same formula as above. One key in one second is 12 wpm, so five keys is 60. Then it measures the spread of those per-second speeds relative to their average (the standard deviation divided by the mean, also called the coefficient of variation) and maps it onto a 0 to 100% scale with a smooth curve. A perfectly even pace scores 100%; very bursty typing drifts toward 0%.

The curve used is:

consistency = 100 x (1 - tanh(c + c³/3 + c⁵/5))
where c = standard deviation / mean of the per-second speeds

Worked example

Over six seconds you press 5, 6, 6, 4, 7 and 5 keys. That gives per-second speeds of 60, 72, 72, 48, 84 and 60 wpm. 

mean = 66
standard deviation ≈ 11.49
c = 11.49 / 66 ≈ 0.174
consistency = 100 x (1 - tanh(0.174 + 0.0018 + 0.00003)) ≈ 82.6%

Now imagine the same typist stops to think for a second in the middle: 60, 0, 84, 36, 96 and 48. The average barely changes, but the spread does, and consistency falls to about 42%.

Other things worth knowing

When the clock starts and stops

The timer starts with your first keypress, not when the page loads, so reading the first word before you start costs you nothing. In word-count and quote tests, the time is measured until you finish the last word. In zen mode, or if you end a long test early, a pause of up to seven seconds after your final keypress is not counted.

Invalid results

The result screen warns you when a test cannot be taken seriously: if it was too short (timed tests under 15 seconds, word tests under 10 words), if you stopped typing for the last five seconds, if accuracy was below 75%, or if the speed was implausibly high. On PuggieType these results are still shown to you, but they do not feed the speed estimate used to place you in Quick Match.

Races use the same rules

In multiplayer, your browser calculates your result with the same code as a solo test. Our server also receives what each player typed, recomputes the speed against the shared word list with the same formula, and flags results that do not match. In timed races, players are ranked by the server's number.

What to do with these numbers

Watch accuracy first. Above about 96%, raw and WPM stay close together and speed becomes the thing to train. Below that, slowing down by a few words per minute will often raise your WPM because fewer words are thrown away. The typing tips page turns this into a practice routine, and modes and settings shows which options make the numbers stricter or more forgiving.
