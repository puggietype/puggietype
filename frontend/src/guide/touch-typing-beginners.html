Touch typing: what it is and how to learn it

Last updated November 17, 2026

Touch typing is typing without looking at the keys. All ten fingers start from the home row, each finger looks after its own small group of keys, and you find those keys by feel rather than by sight. This guide explains how it works, then walks you through learning it from your first session to typing everyday text without glancing down.

What touch typing is, and what it isn't

The alternative most people start with is some version of hunt-and-peck: two to four fingers, eyes moving between the keyboard and the screen, and a slightly different movement for the same key depending on where your hand happened to be. Touch typing replaces that with a fixed system. Each key has one finger that presses it, and each finger has a resting place to return to. Because the movements are always the same, your hands can learn them, and your eyes are free to stay on the text.

Touch typing is a technique, not a speed. A careful touch typist at 30 words per minute is still touch typing. Speed tends to follow once the movements are automatic, but the goal while learning is the method: right finger, eyes up, back to the home row.

Why learn touch typing

Plenty of people type quickly with their own system, and if that is you, you may not need to start over. The case for touch typing is less about a number on a test and more about where your attention goes. When your eyes stay on the screen, you see mistakes as you make them, you keep your place in what you are copying, and you can think about what you are writing instead of where the next key is. Hunt-and-peck also keeps your eyes moving between the keyboard and the screen; touch typing lets them stay on the text.

Accuracy is the other gain. When every key has one practiced movement, the mistakes you make tend to be the same few slips, which are easier to fix than random ones.

The cost is a few weeks of being slower than you are now. That part is unavoidable, and knowing it in advance makes it easier not to give up in week two.

How touch typing works

Everything is organized around the home row, the middle row of letters. On a QWERTY keyboard your left fingers rest on a s d f and your right fingers on j k l ;, with your thumbs over the space bar. Most keyboards have a small bump on f and j so your index fingers can find home without looking.

From there, each finger covers a slanted column of keys above and below its home key:

  • the little fingers take the outer columns, plus shift, and on the right, enter and backspace;
  • the ring and middle fingers take one column each;
  • each index finger takes two columns, its own and the one next to it towards the middle, which is why the left index covers g and the right index covers h;
  • the thumbs press the space bar.

After each keystroke the finger returns to its home key. That return is what makes the system work: every reach starts from the same place, so the same key is always the same movement. How to learn touch typing, step by step

The whole process fits in a short list. The week-by-week plan further down fills in the detail.

  1. Find the home position. Index fingers on the f and j bumps, the other fingers beside them, thumbs over the space bar.
  2. Learn which finger owns which key. Keep a finger chart or the on-screen keymap where you can check it without looking down.
  3. Start with the home row only. Practice short words made from home-row letters until you can type them without looking.
  4. Add a few keys at a time. Two to four new keys per step, always mixed with the keys you already know.
  5. Go slowly enough to be accurate. Speed comes from correct movements repeated many times, not from rushing.
  6. Return to the home row after every key. If your fingers drift, feel for the bumps and reset.
  7. Move on to real words. Once you know every letter, practice common words, then capitals, punctuation and numbers.
  8. Practice a little most days. Ten to twenty minutes is enough.
  9. Use it for real typing. Gradually stop falling back on your old method.

Before you start

You need three things: a keyboard you will use for the whole plan, a regular slot of ten to twenty minutes a day, and a decision to stop looking down during practice. A laptop keyboard is fine. Switching between very different keyboards in the first month makes things harder, so if you have a choice, practice on the one you type on most.

Then set up PuggieType for learning. Open the settings (or press esc and search) and turn on the keymap, set to highlight the next key. This puts a keyboard on the screen, so when you forget where a letter is, you look up rather than down. Looking up keeps your hands in position and your eyes near the text. If live speed is on, turn it off for now. Speed is not what you are working on yet.

A week-by-week plan

Each week adds a few keys and builds on the last. Move on when the current stage feels steady, not when the calendar says so. Some people take two weeks over a stage, and that is fine. The order below is a suggestion; what matters is that you add keys gradually and keep revisiting the old ones.

Week 1: the home row

Use custom mode to make your own practice text. Type in home-row letters only, grouped into short nonsense words: "asdf jkl; fjfj dkdk sls; ajaj", and real words made only from those letters, such as "sad", "lad", "flask", "fall", "add" and "salad". Set it to random order so you can't memorize the sequence, and limit it to around 25 words.

Type slowly enough that almost every key is right. Say the letters in your head if it helps. After each key, let the finger settle back on its home key. Do several short runs, rest, and do a few more. By the end of the week you should be able to type any home-row word without glancing down.

Week 2: the index fingers' extra reach

Add g and h, reached by sliding the index fingers one key inward, and two of the easiest top-row keys, e and i, pressed by the middle fingers. Now you can type far more real words: "said", "high", "flies", "dish", "glad", "shield". Keep mixing in week one's words so the home row stays solid.

Week 3: the rest of the top row

Add r t u y for the index fingers, then w o, then q p for the little fingers. Add them in pairs over the week rather than all on the first day. The reach up and back down is a new movement; watch that your hand returns to the home row rather than drifting upward and staying there.

Week 4: the bottom row

Add v b n m first, then c ,, then x ., then z and /. The bottom row feels the most awkward because the fingers curl down and in. At this point you know every letter, so you can leave custom mode behind for part of each session: switch to words mode with 10 or 25 words of common English, and type them at whatever pace keeps you accurate.

Week 5: real words and capitals

Spend most of your time in words mode now. Start using shift properly: hold it with the little finger of the hand that is not typing the letter. A capital "T" is right little finger on shift, left index on t. This feels slow at first and is worth getting right early, because the habit of always using the left shift is hard to break later.

This is also the week to tidy up. On the result screen, the practice words button gives you a test made of the words you got wrong. Use it after most runs.

Week 6: punctuation and numbers

Turn on punctuation for some tests, then numbers. Numbers are the hardest reach on the keyboard and even experienced typists may glance at them, so don't expect them to feel natural yet. Keep them in the mix a couple of times a week from here on.

Weeks 7 and 8: longer text and the keymap off

Try a few quote tests, which have real sentences with natural punctuation, and some 60-second time tests. Then turn the keymap off for a day and see what happens. If you find yourself stuck on a key, turn it back on for a while; there is no prize for removing it early.

What a good practice session looks like

Short and frequent beats long and rare. Ten focused minutes most days usually does more than one long weekend session. A simple shape for each session:

  1. Warm up (2 minutes). A couple of easy runs on keys you already know well.
  2. New keys (5 to 10 minutes). Most of the session goes on the keys you are learning this week, in a custom text, slowly.
  3. Mixed text (2 to 5 minutes). A few runs that combine everything, so new keys get used next to old ones.
  4. Look back (1 minute). Note which keys went wrong most. They become tomorrow's custom text.

Stop when you notice your accuracy sliding or your shoulders tensing up. Practicing while tired mostly trains tired movements. For general guidance on chair, desk and keyboard setup (not medical advice), see the typing posture and desk setup guide.

Common beginner mistakes, and how to fix them

  • Glancing down "just this once". Every glance is a small step back towards the old habit. Look at the on-screen keymap instead, or slow down until you can find the key by feel.
  • Going too fast too early. If more than a few keys per run are wrong, you are practicing mistakes. Drop your pace until nearly every key is right; the guide to improving typing accuracy explains why this pays off.
  • Letting fingers wander. If a finger stays wherever it last pressed, the next reach starts from the wrong place. Let each finger fall back to its home key, and feel for the bumps during pauses.
  • Borrowing the wrong finger. Ring fingers often take over the little fingers' keys, and one index finger may cover for the other near the middle. Check the finger chart when a key keeps going wrong.
  • Judging progress by one test. Single results jump around. Compare your average over a week instead. The WPM and accuracy guide explains what each number on the result screen measures.

How long it takes, and what to expect

Expect weeks rather than days. The plan above runs over about eight weeks of ten to twenty minutes most days, and some people take longer over a stage. How quickly you get back to your old speed depends on how much you practice, how fast you typed before, and how strictly you keep your eyes up. Nobody can promise a particular speed by a particular week, so compare yourself only with your own earlier results.

  • You will get slower before you get faster. For the first few weeks your touch-typing speed will be below your old speed. This is the hardest part of the plan, not a sign that it isn't working.
  • Some keys will lag behind. Most learners have a few keys that stay unreliable for longer than the rest, often on the bottom row or the little fingers. Put them in a custom text on their own.
  • Progress is uneven. A bad day after a good one is normal. Look at how you are doing over a week, not from one test to the next.
  • Your eyes will want to drop. Notice it each time and look at the on-screen keymap instead. That habit is what you are really building.

Once touch typing feels comfortable, speed becomes the thing to work on. The plan to type faster and break plateaus picks up from there.

Moving touch typing into everyday use

The awkward phase is when you can touch type in practice but your old method is still faster for real work. Many people learn the technique and then slide back because a deadline came along. A few ways to avoid that:

  • Pick one kind of everyday typing to do only by touch, such as chat messages or search queries, where speed doesn't matter much. Widen it as you get comfortable.
  • When you have something urgent, it is fine to type it the old way. Just don't let that become the default again.
  • Keep a short daily practice session going until touch typing is faster than your old method. After that, everyday typing becomes practice in its own right.

You know you are there when you stop thinking about which finger to use and only think about the words.

Frequently asked questions

What is touch typing?

Typing without looking at the keyboard. Your fingers start on the home row, each finger presses its own set of keys, and you find keys by feel while your eyes stay on the screen.

How long does it take to learn touch typing?

Usually weeks rather than days. The plan on this page runs over about eight weeks of ten to twenty minutes most days, and some people take longer. You will be slower than your old method at first; compare your own results over a week rather than from one test to the next.

Should I cover my hands?

Some people find a cloth or a box over the keyboard helps break the habit of looking. It works, but the on-screen keymap does the same job more gently, because you still get help when you are lost.

Is it too late to learn?

No. The method is the same at any age. Adults often find the first week frustrating because they are used to being competent, and that feeling is the main obstacle, not the learning itself.

Do I need a special keyboard?

No. Any standard keyboard works, including the one you already use. It helps to practice on the keyboard you type on most, and to avoid switching between very different keyboards while you learn.

Can I learn touch typing on a laptop?

Yes. Laptop keyboards use the same layout and finger positions. The keys are often flatter and closer together, so take a moment at the start of each session to find the home row by feel.

Should I learn speed or accuracy first?

Accuracy. Correct movements repeated many times turn into speed on their own, while fast, careless practice builds mistakes into the habit.

Should I switch to a different layout while I'm learning anyway?

It is a reasonable moment to consider it, since you are starting from scratch either way. Read the keyboard layouts guide first: there are real costs, such as shortcuts and shared computers, that are easy to underestimate.
