Learning touch typing from zero: a week-by-week plan

Last updated November 10, 2026

Touch typing means typing without looking at the keyboard, with each finger responsible for its own keys. If you currently hunt for keys or use two or three fingers, this plan takes you from your first session to typing everyday text by feel, one small step at a time.

Why learn it at all

Plenty of people type quickly with their own system, and if that is you, you may not need to start over. The case for touch typing is less about a number on a test and more about where your attention goes. When your eyes stay on the screen, you see mistakes as you make them, you keep your place in what you are copying, and you can think about what you are writing instead of where the next key is. Hunt-and-peck typing is also limited by how fast your eyes can move between keyboard and screen; touch typing removes that limit.

The cost is a few weeks of being slower than you are now. That part is unavoidable, and knowing it in advance makes it easier not to give up in week two.

Before week one

You need three things: a keyboard you will use for the whole plan, a regular slot of ten to twenty minutes a day, and a decision to stop looking down during practice. A laptop keyboard is fine. Switching between very different keyboards in the first month makes things harder, so if you have a choice, practice on the one you type on most.

Then set up PuggieType for learning. Open the settings (or press esc and search) and turn on the keymap, set to highlight the next key. This puts a keyboard on the screen, so when you forget where a letter is, you look up rather than down. Looking up keeps your hands in position and your eyes near the text. If live speed is on, turn it off for now. Speed is not what you are working on yet.

Finally, find the home position: left fingers on a s d f, right fingers on j k l ;, thumbs resting on the space bar. Feel for the small ridges on f and j. The home row and finger placement guide has a full table of which finger presses which key; keep it open in another tab for the first few weeks.

The plan

Each week adds a few keys and builds on the last. Move on when the current stage feels steady, not when the calendar says so. Some people take two weeks over a stage, and that is fine. The order below is a suggestion; what matters is that you add keys gradually and keep revisiting the old ones.

Week 1: the home row

Use custom mode to make your own practice text. Type in home-row letters only, grouped into short nonsense words: "asdf jkl; fjfj dkdk sls; ajaj", and real words made only from those letters, such as "sad", "lad", "flask", "fall", "add" and "salad". Set it to random order so you can't memorize the sequence, and limit it to around 25 words.

Type slowly enough that almost every key is right. Say the letters in your head if it helps. After each key, let the finger settle back on its home key. Do several short runs, rest, and do a few more. By the end of the week you should be able to type any home-row word without glancing down.

Week 2: the index fingers' extra reach

Add g and h, reached by sliding the index fingers one key inward, and two of the easiest top-row keys, e and i, pressed by the middle fingers. Now you can type far more real words: "said", "high", "flies", "dish", "glad", "shield". Keep mixing in week one's words so the home row stays solid.

Week 3: the rest of the top row

Add r t u y for the index fingers, then w o, then q p for the little fingers. Add them in pairs over the week rather than all on the first day. The reach up and back down is a new movement; watch that your hand returns to the home row rather than drifting upward and staying there.

Week 4: the bottom row

Add v b n m first, then c ,, then x ., then z and /. The bottom row feels the most awkward because the fingers curl down and in. At this point you know every letter, so you can leave custom mode behind for part of each session: switch to words mode with 10 or 25 words of common English, and type them at whatever pace keeps you accurate.

Week 5: real words and capitals

Spend most of your time in words mode now. Start using shift properly: hold it with the little finger of the hand that is not typing the letter. A capital "T" is right little finger on shift, left index on t. This feels slow at first and is worth getting right early, because the habit of always using the left shift is hard to break later.

This is also the week to tidy up. On the result screen, the practice words button gives you a test made of the words you got wrong. Use it after most runs.

Week 6: punctuation and numbers

Turn on punctuation for some tests, then numbers. Numbers are the hardest reach on the keyboard and many experienced typists still glance at them, so don't expect them to feel natural yet. Keep them in the mix a couple of times a week from here on.

Weeks 7 and 8: longer text and the keymap off

Try a few quote tests, which have real sentences with natural punctuation, and some 60-second time tests. Then turn the keymap off for a day and see what happens. If you find yourself stuck on a key, turn it back on for a while; there is no prize for removing it early.

What a good session looks like

Short and frequent beats long and rare. Ten focused minutes most days usually does more than one long weekend session. A simple shape for each session is: a minute or two warming up on keys you already know, most of the time on the keys you are learning this week, and a couple of runs at the end mixing everything.

Stop when you notice your accuracy sliding or your shoulders tensing up. Practicing while tired mostly trains tired movements. If your hands, wrists or forearms hurt, stop and read the typing ergonomics guide.

What to expect

  • You will get slower before you get faster. For the first few weeks your touch-typing speed will be below your old speed. This is the hardest part of the plan, not a sign that it isn't working.
  • Some keys will lag behind. Most learners have a few keys that stay unreliable for longer than the rest, often on the bottom row or the little fingers. Put them in a custom text on their own.
  • Progress is uneven. A bad day after a good one is normal. Look at how you are doing over a week, not from one test to the next.
  • Your eyes will want to drop. Notice it each time and look at the on-screen keymap instead. That habit is what you are really building.

Moving touch typing into everyday use

The awkward phase is when you can touch type in practice but your old method is still faster for real work. Many people learn the technique and then slide back because a deadline came along. A few ways to avoid that:

  • Pick one kind of everyday typing to do only by touch, such as chat messages or search queries, where speed doesn't matter much. Widen it as you get comfortable.
  • When you have something urgent, it is fine to type it the old way. Just don't let that become the default again.
  • Keep a short daily practice session going until touch typing is faster than your old method. After that, everyday typing becomes practice in its own right.

You know you are there when you stop thinking about which finger to use and only think about the words.

Questions beginners often ask

Should I cover my hands?

Some people find a cloth or a box over the keyboard helps break the habit of looking. It works, but the on-screen keymap does the same job more gently, because you still get help when you are lost.

Is it too late to learn?

No. The plan is the same at any age. Adults often find the first week frustrating because they are used to being competent, and that feeling is the main obstacle, not the learning itself.

How long does it take to learn to touch type?

Expect weeks rather than days. The plan above runs over about eight weeks of ten to twenty minutes most days, and some people take two weeks over a stage. For the first few weeks you will be slower than your old method. There is no speed you are guaranteed to reach by a given week; compare your own results over a week, not from one test to the next.

Should I switch to a different layout while I'm relearning anyway?

It is a reasonable moment to consider it, since you are starting from scratch either way. Read the keyboard layouts guide first: there are real costs, such as shortcuts and shared computers, that are easy to underestimate.
