Home row and finger placement: which finger presses which key

Touch typing works because every key belongs to one finger, and every finger has a place to come back to. This guide maps out those zones on a standard QWERTY keyboard, explains the keys people argue about, and covers the placement mistakes that hold typists back.

The home row

The middle row of letters is the home row. Your fingers rest there between keystrokes, and every other key is reached from there and then released back to it. On QWERTY, the left hand sits on a s d f and the right hand on j k l ;. The index fingers are on f and j, which have small raised bumps so you can find them without looking. The thumbs hover over the space bar.

"Resting" is a slight exaggeration. Your fingertips touch the keys lightly without pressing them, and your wrists float above the desk rather than resting on it. If you lean the weight of your hands on the home row keys, they are harder to lift and every reach starts from a standstill. Fingers should be curved, like holding a small ball, so that the tips rather than the pads meet the keys.

Finger zones

Each finger owns a column of keys that runs diagonally up and to the left from its home key, because the rows on a normal keyboard are staggered. The table below is the usual assignment for a standard staggered QWERTY keyboard.

left ring 2 w s x
left middle 3 e d c
left index 4 5 r t f g v b
right index 6 7 y u h j n m
right middle 8 i k ,
right ring 9 o l .
right little 0 - = p [ ] \ ; ' /, plus enter, backspace and right shift
thumbs space; often alt and, on a Mac, cmd

You don't have to memorize the table. Type slowly with the right fingers for a week or two and it becomes something your hands know rather than something you look up. PuggieType's on-screen keymap helps while you are learning: you can look at it instead of your hands.

The index fingers do the most work

Each index finger covers two columns instead of one: its own, and the column next to it towards the middle of the keyboard. That gives the left index r t f g v b and the right index y u h j n m. The index fingers are usually the most agile, so this is a sensible division, but it does mean the inner columns are where many errors come from. Mixing up r and t, or n and m, is a common slip because the same finger has to choose between them.

When the index finger reaches for g or h, the rest of the hand should stay put. If your whole hand slides over, the other fingers lose their home keys and the next letter lands in the wrong place.

Shift, enter and backspace

For capital letters, hold shift with the little finger of the opposite hand from the letter. Capital "A" uses right shift and left little finger on a; capital "J" uses left shift and right index on j. Using one shift key for everything forces one hand to stretch across or to do two things at once, which is slow and a common cause of wrong capitals.

enter and backspace both belong to the right little finger. Backspace is a long reach, up and to the right. Many fast typists lift the whole right hand slightly to reach it and then return; that is fine as long as the hand comes straight back to the home row. How often you reach for backspace, and when, is a big part of accuracy, covered in the accuracy guide.

Thumbs and the space bar

Many people press space with one thumb, and that is fine. Some alternate thumbs, using the opposite hand to the last letter of the word. Neither is the "correct" method; what matters is that the other thumb stays relaxed above the bar rather than tucked under your palm, and that pressing space doesn't pull your fingers off the home row.

The keys people argue about

The assignment above is a convention, not a law, and a few keys sit right on the boundary between two fingers. It is worth knowing that there are different schools of thought, so you don't think you are doing it wrong when a chart disagrees with you.

  • 6. Because the number row is shifted further left than the letter rows, 6 sits above and between t and y. Some charts give it to the left index, others to the right. Use whichever feels like less of a stretch, and then always use the same one.
  • b. Traditionally a left-index key, but it sits near the middle of the keyboard, and plenty of good typists use the right index for it. Again, pick one and stick to it.
  • y and t on split keyboards. Split and ergonomic keyboards physically divide the keys, which settles these debates for you: whichever half a key is on decides the hand.
  • Ortholinear and column-staggered keyboards. On keyboards where the keys are in straight columns rather than offset rows, each finger moves straight up and down. The same finger assignments apply, but the reaches feel different; expect some adjustment if you switch.

The real rule underneath all of these is consistency. The point of finger zones is that each key has exactly one movement attached to it. Two different fingers for the same key means two movements to learn and a moment of hesitation every time.

Returning home

The habit that separates touch typists from people who know the finger chart is returning to the home row after every reach. When you type "the", your left index finger goes up to t and comes back to f, even though it only stays there for a moment before the next key. If fingers wander and stay wherever they last pressed, every following reach is measured from the wrong place, and your accuracy suffers in ways that are hard to pin down.

You don't need to consciously press f and j each time. Just let the fingers fall back. Every so often, during a pause, feel for the bumps to check where you are.

Common placement mistakes

  • Hands drifting to the right. Many people slowly creep their right hand one key over, onto k l ; '. If you keep typing "k" for "j" or ";" for "l", check for the bump under your right index.
  • Little fingers left out. It is tempting to let the ring finger take a or p. This works at low speed and gets in the way at high speed, because the ring finger now has too many keys.
  • Flat fingers. With flat fingers the pads land on the keys and the reach to the top row is longer. Curving the fingers makes every reach shorter.
  • Resting the wrists hard. Anchored wrists force the fingers to do all the stretching, especially for the number row. Let the hands float a little.
  • Looking down "just for numbers". The number row is the hardest to learn by feel, and that is exactly why it is worth practicing without looking.

Drills for finger placement

You can build these in PuggieType's custom mode: paste in the text, choose random order, and set a word limit.

  1. One finger at a time. Make a text from a single finger's keys, for example "frf ftf fgf fvf fbf" for the left index. Type it slowly and watch that the other fingers stay on their home keys.
  2. Neighboring keys. Mix keys that the same finger has to choose between: "rt tr rtr ftg gtf" or "nm mn hjn mjh". This targets the inner-column confusion described above.
  3. Capitals with the opposite shift. A text of capitalized words ("Jam Fish Kite Dog Lamp Sand") makes you practice using both shift keys.
  4. Numbers. Turn on numbers in time or words mode, or make a custom text of short number groups. Keep the keymap on until you trust your reach.

Use stop on error set to letter for these drills. It won't let you continue until you press the right key, which makes you find the correct finger instead of typing past the mistake.
