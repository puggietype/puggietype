Keyboard layouts: QWERTY, Dvorak, Colemak and Workman compared

A keyboard layout is just a decision about which letter goes under which finger. Most people never question the one they learned, but a few alternatives have real followings. Here is what each one tries to do, what switching actually costs, and how to test one before you commit.

Layout is not the same as keyboard

It helps to separate two things that often get mixed up. The physical keyboard is the hardware: how many keys it has, how the rows are staggered, whether it is one piece or split in two. The layout is the mapping from those physical keys to characters, and it is set in software. Your operating system decides that pressing the key to the right of caps lock types an "a". Change the setting and the same key can type something else. You do not need a new keyboard to try a new layout.

Layouts also differ by language. Much of Europe uses national variants: France and Belgium use AZERTY, which swaps a with q and z with w; Germany, Austria and Switzerland use QWERTZ, which swaps y and z. These layouts exist mainly to fit accented letters and local punctuation, not to change how efficient English typing is. The rest of this page is about the English-focused alternatives.

The four layouts most people ask about

Layout Home row (left to right) Main idea
QWERTY a s d f g h j k l ; The default almost everywhere. Not designed for modern typing.
Dvorak a o e u i d h t n s Vowels under the left hand, common consonants under the right.
Colemak a r s t d h n e i o Fix QWERTY's worst problems while moving as few keys as possible.
Workman a s h t g y n e o i Like Colemak, but avoids sideways stretches for the index fingers.

QWERTY

QWERTY comes from the typewriters of the 1870s. Its arrangement was shaped by the mechanical needs of those machines and by the habits of the people who sold and used them, not by any study of how fingers move. The result is a layout where several of the most common English letters sit off the home row, and many frequent letter pairs are typed by the same finger or by awkward jumps between rows.

That said, QWERTY's flaws are easy to overstate. Plenty of people type very fast on it, every computer you sit down at already uses it, and every keyboard shortcut and game was designed around it. For most people it is good enough, and the cost of leaving it is real.

Dvorak

The Dvorak Simplified Keyboard was designed by August Dvorak in the 1930s. It puts all the vowels on the left side of the home row and the most common consonants on the right, so typing tends to alternate between hands. It also moves punctuation: the comma, full stop and apostrophe sit at the top left, where QWERTY has q w e.

Dvorak is the most radical of the four. Almost every letter moves, which means almost nothing from your QWERTY habits carries over. The upside is a clean break; the downside is the longest relearning period and the biggest disruption to shortcuts.

Colemak and Colemak-DH

Colemak was released by Shai Coleman in 2006. Its goal is to get most of the benefit of a modern layout while leaving as much of QWERTY alone as possible. The bottom-left keys z x c v stay where they are, as do most punctuation keys and q, w and a. The common letters r s t n e i o move onto the home row. The official version also turns caps lock into an extra backspace.

A common criticism of the original Colemak is that d and h sit in the middle columns of the home row, which means the index fingers reach sideways for them often. Colemak-DH is a popular modification that moves d and h down to the bottom row and brings g and m onto the home row in their place. Many people who learn Colemak today start with DH directly.

Workman

Workman appeared around 2010 and starts from a similar complaint: that reaching sideways into the middle columns is more tiring than reaching up or down. It weighs how comfortable each key position is, and so places letters differently from Colemak even though the two share some ideas. Fewer keys stay in their QWERTY positions than with Colemak; z and x stay put, but c and v each move one key to the right.

And many more

Beyond these four there is a long tail of newer layouts, such as Canary, Graphite, ISRT and BEAKL, usually refinements of the same ideas. For most people, the choice between any two modern layouts matters much less than the choice to switch at all.

What switching actually costs

The honest version: you will be slow for a while. Your QWERTY speed was built over years, and a new layout starts from zero. Most people need weeks of daily practice to reach a speed they can work at comfortably, and longer to catch up with their old one. Some never fully do, and some end up faster. How it goes for you depends on how much you practice and how fast you were to begin with.

Things that catch people out:

  • Shortcuts. ctrl + c, v, x and z are on the bottom left in QWERTY so one hand can reach them. Colemak keeps them there. Workman shifts two of them. Dvorak scatters them across the keyboard. Some operating systems let you keep shortcuts on QWERTY positions while typing in another layout; check yours.
  • Games. Games that use w a s d for movement usually read the physical key, so they keep working. Some read the letter instead, and then your movement keys end up in strange places. You may need to rebind or switch layouts while playing.
  • Other people's computers. At work, at school, or on a friend's laptop, the layout will almost always be QWERTY. You can add a layout on a computer you control in a minute, but not on a locked-down one.
  • Keycap labels. Your keys will show the wrong letters. For touch typists this matters less than you might think, and it is a good push to stop looking down.

Where the remapping happens

The usual way is an operating system setting: add the layout as an input language or keyboard and switch to it. The other way is in the keyboard itself. Many programmable keyboards can store a layout in their firmware, so the keyboard sends the new letters to any computer you plug it into, with the computer still set to QWERTY. That is handy if you move between machines, but it can cause confusion when an operating system or application assumes QWERTY positions for shortcuts. Pick one place to do the remapping, not both, or the two will stack on top of each other.

Keeping QWERTY alive

Some people keep both layouts, switching like two languages, often with a different keyboard for each. For others the old layout fades. If you will regularly need QWERTY, keep practicing it a little, or choose a layout like Colemak that leaves more of it in place.

Who might benefit

A new layout is most worth considering if one of these sounds like you:

  • You are learning touch typing from scratch anyway. You will be slow whichever layout you pick, so the switching cost is much smaller.
  • You type a lot, find QWERTY uncomfortable, and are curious whether moving common letters to the home row helps.
  • You enjoy the project of learning it. That counts for a lot.

It is probably not worth it if your only goal is a higher number on a typing test. Technique, accuracy and regular practice affect speed far more than which layout you use, and very fast typists can be found on all of them. If you have pain when typing, a layout change on its own is unlikely to be the answer; read the ergonomics guide linked below and talk to a professional.

How to switch

Cold turkey

Switch everything at once and stop using QWERTY. Every message you write becomes practice, so it is the fastest route, but the first weeks are frustrating. It works best during a quiet period when you can afford to be slow.

Gradual

Keep QWERTY for real work and practice the new layout for a set time each day until it is fast enough to take over. It is less painful but takes longer, and some people find that constant switching keeps both layouts shaky. If you go this way, set a clear speed at which you will make the full switch, so you do not stay in between forever.

Either way, learn the new layout properly from the home row out, a few keys at a time, rather than hunting for letters. The beginner and finger placement guides below describe that process; it works the same on any layout.

Trying a layout on PuggieType

You can practice another layout here without changing anything on your computer. In the settings:

  • layout emulates another layout inside PuggieType. Leave your system on QWERTY, pick for example colemak_dh, and each key you press is translated by its physical position. It only affects this site. The setting is best left on "default" if you already use the layout at the system level, since emulation can interfere with dead keys and alt layers.
  • keymap mode shows an on-screen keyboard while you type. "next" highlights the key you need to press next, which is useful in the first days of a new layout; "react" lights up what you pressed.
  • keymap layout picks which layout the on-screen keyboard shows. "emulator sync" follows the layout setting above. The keymap style can also be changed to staggered, matrix, split and other shapes to match your keyboard.
  • The layoutfluid funbox switches between several layouts during a single test, for people practicing more than one.

While learning, keep your measuring tests on the same settings each time so you can see real progress, and expect accuracy to matter more than speed for the first few weeks.
