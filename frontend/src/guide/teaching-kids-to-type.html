Teaching kids to type: a guide for parents and teachers

Last updated November 4, 2026

This guide is for adults who want to help a child learn to touch type, at home or in a classroom. It covers how to tell when a child is ready, how to set up a desk that fits them, the habits worth building first, and how to keep practice short and low pressure.

The advice below works with pen and paper, a plain text editor, or tools made for younger learners, and suits learners at many stages.

Signs a child is ready

There's no single right age. Readiness tends to show up in three ways.

Their hands fit the keyboard. A child needs to rest four fingers of each hand on the middle row without stretching. If their hands are very small for a full-size keyboard, they'll drift back to pecking with one or two fingers.

They read comfortably. Typing means reading a word, holding it in mind, and letting the fingers produce it. A child who still sounds out most words is practicing reading, which is worthwhile, but it isn't typing practice yet.

They can put up with feeling slow. Touch typing is awkward for the first few weeks, and usually slower than looking at the keys. A child has to accept that for a while.

If a child gets frustrated quickly, waiting a few months often helps more than pushing on. Older students who already type with two fingers can still switch. They'll just go through a slower stretch before it pays off.

Setting up the space

Most desks and chairs are sized for adults. A child at the family computer often sits too low, with feet dangling and arms raised to reach the keys. Small changes make a real difference:

  • Support the feet. A box, a footrest or a stack of books under dangling feet makes it easier to sit upright.
  • Bring elbows level with the keys. A cushion on the chair, or the keyboard on a lower surface, keeps forearms roughly flat.
  • Try a compact keyboard. Keyboards without a number pad keep both hands closer to the middle of the body. A laptop keyboard can suit small hands too.
  • Raise the screen. Prop a laptop on books and use a separate keyboard so the child isn't bending their neck.

Children fidget, and that's fine. The goal is a setup where a comfortable posture is easy, not perfect stillness. If a child mentions pain in their hands, wrists or neck, pause practice and check with a doctor.

The two habits that matter most

Fingers on the home row

Show the child where the left fingers rest on A, S, D and F and the right fingers on J, K, L and the semicolon, with thumbs on the space bar. Have them close their eyes and feel the small bumps on F and J. "Find home without looking" makes a good opening ritual for every session.

Eyes on the screen

Looking down at the keys is the habit that holds young typists back most, and it's much easier to prevent than to undo. Some families cover the keys with a cloth or blank stickers for a few weeks. A printed keyboard chart taped above the screen gives the child somewhere to glance up to instead of down.

Short sessions, most days

Attention for repetitive practice runs out quickly, and tired fingers make sloppy mistakes. Ten minutes on most days usually does more than one long session at the weekend. For younger children, five minutes is plenty.

Try to stop while things are still going well, so the next session starts on a good note. A simple routine:

  1. Find the home row without looking.
  2. Type a few short lines at an easy pace, such as this week's spelling words.
  3. Finish with something fun, like typing a sentence they made up or a short note to a grandparent.

Accuracy before speed

Children chase whatever number the adults around them react to. Cheer for speed and they'll start mashing keys and peeking at the keyboard. Notice careful typing and they'll build the habit that brings speed later on its own.

Things worth praising:

  • fewer mistakes than last time
  • finding the home row without looking
  • typing a tricky word correctly on the first try
  • carrying on after a mistake instead of giving up

Don't compare children

Siblings and classmates develop at different rates. Hand size, reading level and time spent at a computer all play a part. Comparing one child with another mostly discourages the slower one. Compare each child with their own work from a few weeks ago. Progress tends to come in steps with flat stretches between them, and it helps to tell children that's normal.

Practice ideas away from a typing site

Not all practice needs a screen or an account:

  • Paper keyboard. Print a full-size keyboard and practice finger placement and "which finger presses this key" on the table.
  • Spelling and copywork. Type this week's spelling list or copy a paragraph from a favorite book into a plain document.
  • Letters and lists. Shopping lists, thank-you notes and short stories give typing a purpose.
  • School-approved tools. Many schools already use typing programs built for younger students. Ask the teacher what the class uses so practice at home matches.
  • Games, in moderation. Typing games make repetition feel like play, but many reward raw speed, which encourages looking at the keys. If a child's eyes keep dropping during a game, switch back to slower, careful practice for a while.
