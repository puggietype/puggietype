How to type faster: practical tips and a practice plan

Typing speed improves the same way any motor skill does: good habits, short regular practice, and honest measurement. Here is what works, and how to use PuggieType to do it.

Set up before you start

Posture

Sit with your feet flat and the keyboard low enough that your elbows rest at roughly a right angle by your sides. Your wrists should float in a straight line with your forearms rather than bending up or resting hard on the desk edge. Put the screen at eye level so you are not hunching to read the words. None of this feels important during a 30-second test, but it is what lets you practice every day without sore wrists or shoulders.

The home row

Rest your left fingers on a s d f and your right fingers on j k l ;, thumbs on the space bar. The small bumps on f and j let you find this position without looking. Each finger is responsible for the keys directly above and below its home key; the index fingers also cover the column next to them (g, h and the keys above and below). After each key, the finger returns home.

If you already type quickly with your own system, you don't have to start over. But if you are below about 40 words per minute, or you look at the keyboard, learning proper finger placement will raise your ceiling more than anything else on this page. Expect your speed to drop for a week or two while you relearn; that is the price of a much higher ceiling.

Stop looking at the keys

Every glance at the keyboard costs you your place in the text. If you need a reminder of where keys are, turn on the on-screen keymap in the settings so you can look up instead of down.

Accuracy first, then speed

On PuggieType, a word with one wrong letter adds nothing to your WPM, and every wrong keypress lowers your accuracy even after you fix it. (The WPM and accuracy guide explains why.) A mistake therefore costs you three times: the wasted keypress, the backspace, and the retyped letter. That is why slowing down slightly often makes you faster.

A useful rule of thumb: if your accuracy is below 95%, you are practicing mistakes. Slow down until you are consistently at 96% or above, then let speed come up on its own. Watch the gap between your WPM and raw speed on the result screen. When it is small, you are typing cleanly.

Settings that enforce accuracy while you practice:

  • min accuracy set to 95% fails the test as soon as you drop below it, so you can't ignore a sloppy run.
  • stop on error: letter makes you type the correct key before you can continue. It builds the habit of fixing mistakes immediately instead of noticing them three words later.
  • difficulty: master ends the test on the first mistake. It is hard, but a few rounds teach you to type deliberately.

Read ahead and keep a rhythm

Fast typists read one or two words ahead of where they are typing, so their fingers never wait for their eyes. Try to keep your eyes on the next word while you finish the current one. Pair this with an even rhythm: steady typing at a moderate pace beats fast bursts followed by pauses. The consistency number on the result screen tells you how even your pace was, and the speed chart shows exactly where you slowed down.

Turning on blind mode for a few tests helps with this, too. With mistakes hidden, you stop reacting to every slip and learn to keep going.

A practice plan

Fifteen to twenty minutes a day, most days, works better than an hour once a week. Here is a routine that fits in that time.

  1. Warm up, 2 minutes. Zen mode or a couple of 15-second tests at an easy pace. Focus on posture and returning to the home row.
  2. Accuracy block, 5 minutes. Words mode, 25 words, with min accuracy at 96%. Restart with tab then enter as many times as you need. The goal is clean runs, not fast ones.
  3. Weak spots, 5 minutes. On the result screen, use practice words to retype the words you missed. Or collect your trouble words into a custom test (custom mode, random order) and type them until they feel easy.
  4. Push, 3 minutes. Set the pace caret to a custom speed about 5 to 10 wpm above your usual result and try to stay with it on a few 30-second tests. It is fine to lose; the point is to stretch.
  5. Measure, 2 minutes. End with one or two 60-second tests at your normal pace and note the WPM and accuracy. Compare that number week to week, not test to test.

Once a week, add punctuation and numbers, or switch to quote mode. Real text has capitals, commas and longer words, and practicing on it closes the gap between your test speed and your speed when writing email or code.

Use races as practice

Racing another person pushes most people a few words per minute above their solo pace. Quick Match pairs you with players near your speed, so races are close; a private room with friends is good motivation for regular practice. Racing is better for stretching your speed than for building accuracy, so mix it with the solo routine above. The multiplayer guide explains how matching works.

Common mistakes

  • Taking only short tests. 15-second scores are always higher and say little about your real speed. Use them for warm-ups.
  • Chasing a single record. One lucky test isn't progress. Your typical result over many tests is what counts.
  • Practicing tired. When accuracy starts slipping, stop for the day. Practicing tired fingers mostly practices mistakes.
  • Changing everything at once. Switching layout, keyboard and technique in the same week makes it impossible to know what helped.

What progress looks like

Most people who practice regularly gain a few words per minute in the first weeks, then progress slows and comes in steps rather than a steady line. Plateaus are normal and usually break when you go back to accuracy work for a while. Keep the settings the same for your measuring tests so you are comparing like with like.
