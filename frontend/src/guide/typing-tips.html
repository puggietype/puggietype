Typing tips: a 15-minute daily practice routine

Last updated November 4, 2026

Most people who want to type better don't need a long program. They need a short routine they will actually do on most days. This page is that routine, plus the handful of habits that make it work. If you want a longer plan for breaking a plateau, see the structured plan to type faster. If you are learning to touch type from scratch, start with touch typing for beginners and come back here once the home row feels familiar.

Three habits to have in place first

These have their own guides, so here is only the short version.

  • Sit comfortably and type lightly. Short practice every day only works if it doesn't hurt. Typing ergonomics covers chair, desk and keyboard setup.
  • Keep your fingers on the home row and your eyes on the screen. If you still look down, turn on the on-screen keymap in the settings so you can look up instead. Home row and finger placement shows which finger owns which key.
  • Accuracy before speed. A word with a wrong letter adds nothing to your WPM, so clean typing is usually faster typing. How to improve typing accuracy goes deeper.

The routine

Fifteen minutes, most days. Do the blocks in this order: each one sets up the next.

1. Warm up (2 minutes). Open zen mode, or take two or three 15-second tests at an easy pace. Don't look at the score. Check that your shoulders are loose and your fingers come back to the home row after each word.

2. Accuracy block (5 minutes). Switch to words mode with 25 words and set min accuracy to 96% in the settings. A test that drops below that fails straight away, so restart with tab then enter and go again. Aim for three clean runs in a row rather than a fast one. If that feels easy, try stop on error: letter for a few runs, which makes you fix each mistake before moving on.

3. Weak spots (5 minutes). After a test, the result screen lets you open practice words to retype the words you missed. Do that for two or three tests. If the same words keep coming up, paste them into custom mode, set it to random order, and type them until they stop feeling awkward.

4. Push (2 minutes). Set the pace caret to a custom speed about 5 to 10 wpm above your usual result. Take a few 30-second tests and try to stay with the caret. Losing is fine. The point is to give your hands a pace they haven't settled into yet.

5. Measure (1 minute). Finish with one 60-second test at your normal pace. Write down the WPM and accuracy.

How to keep track

Use the same setup for every measuring test, for example time mode, 60 seconds, English, no punctuation. Changing the setup changes the score, and then you can't tell whether you improved.

Keep a simple note with the date, WPM, accuracy and one line on how it felt. Once a week, look at the last seven entries together. A single test can swing by several words per minute for no reason, so the weekly picture is the one that matters. Your saved results stay in your browser, so a note also keeps your history if you switch devices.

Adjusting the routine

  • Accuracy below 95% most days: make the accuracy block 8 minutes and drop the push block until it improves.
  • Accuracy steady at 97% or more: take a minute from the accuracy block and give it to the push block.
  • The same few letters keep failing: spend the weak-spots block on those letters in custom mode, mixed into real words.
  • Bored: once a week, swap the measure block for quote mode or turn on punctuation and numbers. Real text has capitals and commas, and practicing it narrows the gap between test speed and everyday writing.

Racing as part of practice

Racing another person tends to pull people a little above their solo pace, which makes it a good stand-in for the push block now and then. Quick Match pairs you with players near your speed, and a private room lets you race friends with a link. Multiplayer is for ages 13 and over. Racing builds speed more than accuracy, so keep the accuracy block on race days too. The multiplayer guide explains how matching works.

Common mistakes

  • Only taking 15-second tests. Short tests read high and say little about your real speed. Use them to warm up.
  • Chasing one record. A lucky test isn't progress. Your typical result is.
  • Practicing tired. When accuracy starts slipping and won't come back, stop for the day.
  • Changing everything at once. A new layout, a new keyboard and a new technique in the same week make it impossible to know what helped.

What to expect

People who follow a routine like this usually gain a few words per minute in the first weeks. After that, progress tends to come in steps, with flat stretches in between. Flat stretches are normal. When one lasts more than two or three weeks, the structured plan to type faster has ways to find out why.
