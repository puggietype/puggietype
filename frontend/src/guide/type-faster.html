A structured plan to type faster and break plateaus

Last updated November 4, 2026

Short daily practice gets most people moving. This page is for the next stage: when random tests stop making you faster and you want a plan that runs over weeks, tells you what to work on, and helps you get unstuck.

Why random tests stop working

Taking test after test feels like practice, and at first it is. Early on, almost any typing makes you better because everything is new. After a while, though, repeating the same 30-second test mostly rehearses the habits you already have, good and bad. Your fingers settle into a pace that feels comfortable, and improvement tends to stall there.

Getting faster past that point needs three things that random tests don't give you: a reliable way to measure progress, practice aimed at a specific weakness, and a deliberate change of pace now and then so your body has something new to adapt to. The plan below is built around those three.

Week one: measure a baseline

Before changing anything, find out where you are. A single test is a poor measurement: one good run with easy words can be several words per minute above your normal, and one distracted run can be well below. What you want is your typical result.

  1. Pick one measuring setup and write it down: for example, time mode, 60 seconds, English, no punctuation or numbers. Use exactly this setup for every measurement from now on.
  2. On three or four days this week, take five measuring tests in a row after a short warm-up.
  3. For each session, note the middle result of the five (the median), not the best. Note the accuracy of that same test too.

The median is barely affected by one lucky or unlucky run, so it mostly moves when you actually change. A simple log is enough: date, median WPM, accuracy, and a few words on how it felt. Over weeks this log is what tells you whether the plan is working, because day-to-day noise hides slow progress.

While you are measuring, also look at the details. Open the words history on the result screen and hover over words to see how fast you typed each one. Turn on the burst heatmap setting and the history is colored by speed, so slow words stand out. Make a short list of the words and letter combinations that keep showing up as slow or wrong. That list is the raw material for the drills below.

The training cycle

After the baseline week, train in repeating three-week cycles. Each week has a different emphasis, and you keep a short measuring session at the end of every practice day so the log stays up to date.

Week Focus What most of your practice looks like
1. Technique clean, relaxed movement Slow, accurate drills on your weak list. Speed does not matter this week.
2. Stretch going faster than comfortable Short bursts with the pace caret above your usual speed, then easy recovery tests.
3. Consolidate making the new speed normal Longer tests and real text at a pace just under your stretch speed.

The point of the cycle is that each kind of practice prepares the next. Technique work fixes the movements that break when you speed up. Stretch work shows your hands that a higher speed is possible, even if it is messy. Consolidation turns that brief higher speed into something you can hold for a minute or more with good accuracy. Then you go back to technique, because the faster speed will have exposed new weak spots.

Your measurements will often dip during a stretch week. That is expected: you are practicing at a pace your hands can't yet control. Judge each cycle by the median at the end of the consolidation week, compared with the end of the previous cycle.

Drills worth doing

Letter pairs and triples

A small set of letter pairs ("th", "er", "in", "ou") and triples ("the", "ing", "ion") turns up in a large share of English words. If one of these is slow or awkward for you, it slows down every word that contains it. Custom mode is the tool for this: write a line of the pair or triple you want to train, mixed with short words that contain it, set the order to random, and type it in short sets.

For example, if "ck" keeps tripping you up, a custom text such as back neck pick stuck lock check quick track gives you many repetitions in a minute. Do a few sets until it feels smooth, then go back to normal tests and see whether those words have stopped standing out in the history.

Chunking common words

Fast typists tend not to spell out common words letter by letter. Words like "the", "and", "that" and "with" become a single movement, fired off as one unit. You can encourage this by drilling the most frequent short words on their own until you no longer think about their letters. A custom text of the twenty or so words you use most, in random order, works well. When a word stops feeling like separate keypresses, it's chunked.

Overspeed bursts

In stretch weeks, set the pace caret to a custom speed clearly above your median and take 15-second tests trying to stay with it. Accept the mistakes. After three or four bursts, take one test at a normal, relaxed pace. Many people find this recovery test feels easier than usual, and sometimes it is faster. You can also set the pace caret to your average or personal best to race your own history instead of a fixed number.

Endurance

Short tests flatter everyone. If your 15-second results are far above your 60-second ones, your speed fades under sustained effort. In consolidation weeks, include 120-second tests and longer quotes. Watch the speed chart on the result screen: a line that slopes down over the test means you are starting too fast or tensing up. Aim for a flat line, even if it sits a little lower.

Real text

At least once a week, practice on text that looks like what you actually type: quotes, punctuation and numbers turned on, or a custom text pasted from your own writing or code. Speed on plain lowercase words doesn't fully carry over to capitals, commas and symbols, and this is where many people's real-world typing is slowest.

Diagnosing a plateau

A plateau is several weeks in which your median doesn't move. There is usually a cause, and the result screen often points to it. Work through these questions in order.

Is accuracy holding you back?

Compare your WPM with your raw speed. If raw is well above WPM, your fingers are already fast enough; it's the mistakes that cost you. Spend a full cycle on accuracy before anything else. The accuracy guide covers how to find and fix your error patterns.

Is it one key, finger or combination?

Look at the words history over several tests. If the errors and slow words share letters, the problem is local. Common culprits are the weaker fingers on the outer keys, reaches to the number row, and letter pairs typed by the same finger. Drill those combinations specifically, and check whether you are using the finger you think you are using for them. Stay on the layout you already type on while you do this. Switching layouts restarts that muscle memory, and it rarely breaks a plateau by itself. The keyboard layouts guide is for choosing between QWERTY, Dvorak, Colemak and Workman on purpose, not as a shortcut around a weak pair.

Is your rhythm uneven?

On the speed chart, the raw line shows how fast you typed in each second. If it jumps up and down a lot, and your consistency number is low, you are typing in bursts and pauses. The pauses usually happen at harder words that you didn't see coming. Practice reading slightly further ahead, and try a few tests at a deliberately even, slower pace until the line flattens out.

Are you tired or tense?

If your results drop through a session, or are worse late in the day, the plateau may simply be fatigue. Tension is the other common cause: pressing harder and gripping with your shoulders as you push for speed. Practice shorter sessions, shake out your hands between tests, and pay attention to whether you are holding your breath during hard runs.

Breaking the plateau

  • Change the stimulus. If you've been doing the same drills for weeks, they have stopped teaching you anything. Switch word lists (a larger English list adds less common words), change test length, or move to quotes for a while.
  • Go slower on purpose. A week of deliberately slow, perfect typing often clears out sloppy movements that crept in while you chased speed.
  • Isolate the weak spot. A plateau is often one or two bad combinations dragging everything down. Ten minutes a day on just those often does more than an hour of general tests.
  • Take a break. Skills often settle while you rest. A few days off, or a week of only light, easy typing, is a legitimate part of training, not a failure.

Rest and lighter weeks

Every few cycles, take a lighter week: fewer, shorter sessions with no stretch work, just easy tests and real text. This gives your hands and forearms a rest and often lets the gains from the previous weeks show up in your measurements. If anything hurts, as opposed to just feeling tired, stop and read our typing ergonomics guide; persistent pain is a reason to see a professional, not to push through.

What to expect

Progress is fastest at the start and slows as you get better. Later gains come in steps: weeks of no change, then a jump. Your log will show this more clearly than your memory, which tends to remember only the best runs. Everyone's ceiling and pace of improvement differ, so compare yourself with your own log rather than with anyone else's scores. Steady, varied practice keeps most people improving for a long time.
