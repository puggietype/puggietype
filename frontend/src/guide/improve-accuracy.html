How to improve typing accuracy

Last updated November 2, 2026

Accuracy is often the quickest part of typing to improve, and it usually pays off twice: fewer mistakes and, over time, a higher speed. This guide is about finding the specific mistakes you make and building habits that stop them.

Why accuracy comes first

On PuggieType a word only adds to your WPM if every letter in it is right, and every wrong keypress lowers your accuracy even if you fix it straight away. The WPM and accuracy guide shows the exact rules. The practical consequence is simple: a typo is never free. You pay for the wrong key, for the backspace, and for typing the letter again, and if you don't fix it, you lose the whole word.

There is a second, less obvious reason. Whatever you repeat, you get better at, including mistakes. If you regularly hit r when you mean t, every test reinforces that movement. Practicing accurately is how you make the correct movement the automatic one.

Habits that keep accuracy high

  • Type at a pace you control. The right speed for practice is the one where mistakes are occasional surprises, not a constant stream. If you are fixing something every few words, slow down until you aren't.
  • Keep an even rhythm. Many errors happen when you speed up: a burst on an easy word, then a stumble on the next. A steady, slightly slower beat gives each finger time to land.
  • Stay relaxed. Tense fingers overshoot and press neighboring keys. Let your hands rest lightly on the home row between words, and drop your shoulders.
  • Look at the text, not your hands. Looking down loses your place and makes you guess when you look back up. If you are unsure where a key is, the on-screen keymap is a better reminder.
  • Finish the word you are on. Many errors happen at the end of words, when your attention has already jumped to the next one. Think of the space as part of the word, not the start of the next.

Find your error patterns

Everyone's mistakes are different, but most people make the same few over and over. Practice aimed at your own patterns usually helps more than general practice. PuggieType gives you several ways to see them.

Words history

After a test, open the words history on the result screen. Words you got wrong are marked, and you can see exactly what you typed in place of the target letters. Over five or ten tests, patterns appear: the same letter swapped, the same word mistyped, errors clustering on one hand.

The error marks on the chart

The speed chart on the result screen marks the seconds in which you made errors. If they bunch up at the start, you are rushing out of the gate. If they bunch up at the end, you are tiring or losing focus. If they follow the spikes in your raw speed, you make mistakes whenever you speed up.

Practice words

The practice words option on the result screen builds a new test from the words you missed, the words you typed slowly, or both. It can also include the word before each missed one, because sometimes the mistake comes from the transition between words rather than the word itself.

A short error log

Keep a note of the mistakes you see repeatedly, written as "typed X instead of Y". After a week, sort them into the types below. This takes a couple of minutes and tells you which drills will help most.

Common types of error and what to do about them

Error Example Usual cause and fix
transposed letters "teh" for "the" One hand runs ahead of the other. Drill the word slowly with an even beat between letters, then bring the speed up.
neighboring key "tje" for "the" The finger lands off-center or the wrong finger is used. Check which finger should own the key and practice the reach from the home row.
doubled or dropped letter "leter" or "lettter" Losing count on double letters. Treat a double as one deliberate "tap tap" and drill words like letter, coffee, little, happy.
capitals and shift "THe" or "the" for "The" Shift released too late or pressed too late. Use the shift key on the opposite hand to the letter and release it as the letter goes down.
space timing "thecat" or "th ecat" The thumb fires early or late. Slow down at word boundaries and make the space a clear, separate beat.
autopilot words "there" for "their" Your fingers type a familiar word instead of the one on screen. Read each word fully; drill pairs of similar words in random order.

Custom mode turns any of these into a drill. Put the problem words or letter pairs into a custom text, set it to random order, and type short sets until they come out clean several times in a row. Then return to normal tests and check whether the pattern has faded from your words history.

Using backspace well

Fix now or keep going?

On a typing test, a word with a mistake adds nothing to your WPM, so it usually makes sense to fix an error while you are still in that word: a backspace or two is cheap. Once you have moved on, the calculation changes. Going back to a previous word costs several keypresses and breaks your rhythm, and the original mistake still counts against accuracy. In a timed test, it is often better to accept the lost word and keep going.

In everyday writing the priorities are different, since the final text has to be right. Even there, it is usually faster to finish your sentence and then fix errors than to stop at each one.

Delete whole words

If a word has gone badly wrong, ctrl + backspace (option + backspace on a Mac) deletes it in one press and lets you start the word again. This is faster and calmer than tapping backspace several times while counting letters.

Notice when you are over-correcting

Some people press backspace by reflex, sometimes deleting letters that were correct. If you notice this, you are probably reacting to the feeling of a mistake rather than to what is on the screen. A few tests in blind mode, where mistakes are hidden, can help break the reflex.

Settings that change how backspace works

  • confidence mode: on stops you going back to previous words. You can still fix the current word.
  • confidence mode: max disables backspace entirely. Every keypress is final, which makes you slow down to the speed you can actually type cleanly.
  • stop on error: letter won't accept the next key until you type the correct one, so a mistake can never travel further than one letter.
  • stop on error: word lets you finish the word but won't let you move on until it is right. It trains you to check each word as you complete it.

Use one of these for a few sessions at a time rather than permanently. The aim is to change a habit, then go back to normal settings and see if it stuck.

A one-week accuracy block

  1. Day 1: take ten normal tests and build your error log from the words history.
  2. Days 2 to 4: after a short warm-up, spend most of the session on custom-text drills for your two most common error types, then a few tests with stop on error: word.
  3. Day 5: tests with confidence mode: max at a pace where you rarely miss.
  4. Days 6 and 7: normal settings. Compare your accuracy and words history with day 1.

Speed may dip during the block and often recovers over the following weeks. If it doesn't, check whether new error types have replaced the old ones and run the block again with those.

The mental side

Accuracy is as much about attention as about fingers. Mistakes multiply when you get frustrated: one typo makes you rush to catch up, which causes the next. When you make a mistake, let it go and keep your rhythm. If a test falls apart, restart it calmly instead of pushing harder. And stop for the day when your accuracy keeps sliding: tired practice mostly practices errors.
